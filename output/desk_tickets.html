Reflection — Running the Campus Customs Agent Team

This is my own reflection after actually running the five-agent team against all three tickets (run IDs 3cbd9263-b53b-4c56-bee0-324c3d3ba2e5, f55bd0c5-1183-4d6d-81be-aecad741fd10, and 6f41a5a7-1a2b-46f1-a65d-9e421f532759), approving two real payments, and watching one ticket resolve while two stayed open. I'm writing this from the actual audit trail and database state, not from how I imagined the system would behave when I wrote the Expected plans in Problem 6.

1. Performance by ticket

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

Inventory and Accounting did exactly the job I'd want from a competent first pass: Inventory confirmed the size-S stock was genuinely zero and, critically, called check_vendor_payment_status on its own initiative to catch that Bulldog Print Co had an unpaid invoice — that's the one fact that actually determines what happens next, and the agent found it without being told to look for it. Accounting then confirmed the $3,400 balance covered the $840 invoice. That's a correct, non-trivial analytical chain.

But I want to be honest about what didn't happen: Accounting never called create_payment_approval_request. It recommended the payment in prose — "a human may consider approving the exact $840 payment" — and stopped there. That's actually the safer failure mode (no unauthorized financial artifact was created), but it meant I had to manually create the approval request myself, using the agent's own numbers, before I could even ask for human sign-off. If I'm grading this team on "did it move the ticket toward resolution," the honest answer is: it did the analysis, but it left the very last procedural step — formalizing its own recommendation into an actionable request — undone. I don't think that's a bug in the orchestration (the architecture never promised agents would auto-create approval requests), but it's a real gap in what this specific Accounting prompt actually produces versus what I assumed it would produce when I wrote its system prompt.

The final status, OPEN, is justified and not a failure of the agents: paying the invoice only clears Bulldog Print Co's eligibility to receive a new order. Nobody — not Inventory, not Accounting, not me — can shorten the vendor's 5-day lead time, and no tool in this system can even place that order, since there's no MCP tool for creating a vendor shipment and the shop's own rules forbid contacting vendors directly. The ticket is correctly open because the customer still doesn't have a shirt.

Ticket 102 — Rent

This is the one ticket where I'd give the team close to full marks. Boss called get_ticket_102_rent_and_cash_info itself — one tool call that returns the lease, the due date, the shop date, and the cash balance together — and realized there was nothing left to investigate before deciding whether to delegate. It delegated once, to Accounting, which re-confirmed the same facts and recommended payment. Two agents, one real tool call each, one clean decision. I approved the $2,400 payment, the system executed it through record_approved_payment, and I called close_ticket myself once I'd independently confirmed via a fresh database query that the rent really was paid in full and nothing else was outstanding on that lease.

I don't think this ticket shows the team "performing well" in some deep multi-agent sense — it shows that when the underlying business problem is simple (one number, one due date, one account), four extra agents add nothing. The real intelligence here was in the Boss's tool-calling decision, not in any division of labor across specialists.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount

This run is the most interesting one, and also the one with the most genuine inefficiency. Inventory confirmed 8 of 20 size-M hoodies available. Then Boss delegated to Accounting for a margin/discount opinion — and Accounting did something I didn't script into any example: it used its real escalate_to_boss tool, explicitly stating that "stock and pricing/discount decisions" were "outside Accounting's permitted financial-payment role" and that it couldn't authorize a discount. That's the Problem 5 connectivity correction actually firing in a live run, not a demo I staged — Accounting genuinely recognized the limits of its own role and handed the question back rather than guessing.

The inefficiency: Boss then delegated to Inventory a second time, asking it to re-verify the exact same size-M stock figure it had already reported. That's a real, measurable waste — one extra model call and one extra MCP call (get_ticket_103_pricing_and_inventory_info, called three times total across the run by three different agents) producing a fact the system already had. The delegation-pair cap (max 2 repeats, max 4 hops total) meant this couldn't spiral further, but it still cost real tokens to re-derive something already known.

Analytically, the team nailed the two things that matter — confirmed quantity (8, not 20) and confirmed unit economics ($22 cost / $58 list, $36 margin) — but it could not and did not resolve the ticket, because no amount of internal analysis substitutes for Yale AI Club actually accepting a smaller quantity or a specific discount. OPEN is the right call here too.

The repeated Inventory call is a straightforward inefficiency the plan didn't anticipate and that I wouldn't want to defend as "fine" — it's wasted work.

3. Single agent vs. multi-agent

Ticket 102 is the clearest example in this whole exercise of multi-agent overhead costing more than it bought. A single agent with the full MCP toolset could have called get_ticket_102_rent_and_cash_info, checked that $3,400 covers $2,400, called create_payment_approval_request itself, and stopped — one agent turn instead of two agent starts, one delegation record, and the associated token overhead of a second model call just to have Accounting re-confirm numbers the Boss already had in context. The actual run's Boss→Accounting hop added a second LLM round-trip to re-derive the same lease and cash facts the Boss's own tool call had already surfaced.

Ticket 103's second Inventory call is the same problem in sharper relief: that's not a different agent adding a different skill, it's the same specialist being asked the same question again, purely because of how the delegation graph unfolded. A single agent can't ask itself the same question twice in that wasteful way, because there's no delegation boundary for it to lose track of state across.

Where I still think multiple agents earned their keep: Accounting's refusal to authorize a discount on Ticket 103, and its structural inability to ever call record_approved_payment on its own on any ticket. Those aren't things a single, more "capable" agent would reliably self-impose — they're boundaries baked into which MCP tools each prompt is told it's allowed to use and how its role is defined. A single do-everything agent with the full toolset and no role separation is one unlucky prompt away from deciding it's qualified to authorize a discount, or worse, from talking itself into believing it has implicit permission to call record_approved_payment with a guessed approval_id. The separation isn't really about dividing analytical labor efficiently — Ticket 102 proves that part can be redundant — it's about making it structurally awkward for any one agent to accumulate both the judgment and the authority to move money. And to be clear: that separation is not what actually stopped an unauthorized payment in this system. What actually stopped it was that record_approved_payment requires a real `approval_id` tied to a human-approved record, and no agent anywhere has a tool to grant that status to itself. Multiple agents made the design cleaner to reason about; they were never the security control. The security control was that the approval mechanism exists completely outside any agent's reach.

4. Three new problems the current team could solve

A. A second vendor develops payment problems

Scenario: Elm City Gifts (vendor 2) accumulates an unpaid invoice the same way Bulldog Print Co did, and a ticket comes in asking whether the shop can order more mugs or small goods from them.

Agents: Inventory (stock check), Accounting (invoice/cash check).

Tools: check_vendor_payment_status (with vendor_id=2) to discover the unpaid invoice, get_cash_balance to check whether paying it is affordable, create_payment_approval_request to formally propose the payment.

Outcome: Exactly the same pattern as Ticket 101 — the team can correctly identify the blocker and propose clearing it, but cannot place a new order itself. The narrower, honest outcome is "payment proposal prepared and the vendor-eligibility question answered," not "more mugs ordered."

B. A second lease or rent notice arrives

Scenario: A second rent notice comes in for a different lease (or the same lease, a different month), with a tighter deadline than Ticket 102's two days.

Agents: Facilities (urgency), Accounting (affordability).

Tools: get_shop_date and the lease fields already exposed by a ticket-specific tool, plus get_cash_balance and create_payment_approval_request.

Outcome: The team could flag urgency correctly (it already proved it can compute "days until due" against the real shop date, not the OS clock) and prepare a payment request — but, same as every financial ticket, actual payment requires a human to run the real approval step. With a $160 post-Problem-9 balance, this is also the scenario most likely to force a genuinely difficult "we may not be able to afford this" answer, which Accounting is well-positioned to surface honestly since it checks the real balance rather than assuming funds exist.

C. A customer disputes a prior order's size or quantity

Scenario: A customer claims they were shorted inventory on a past order and wants it investigated.

Agents: Inventory, possibly escalating to Customer Service for drafting a response.

Tools: list_all_tickets to find the original ticket, the relevant ticket-specific tool or inventory lookups to verify what was actually in stock and shipped.

Outcome: The team can verify or refute the factual claim using real inventory records and draft a recommended response. It cannot issue a refund (no such tool exists) and cannot send the response to the customer — only prepare the factual basis and a draft for a human to act on.

5. Three new problems the current team could not solve

A. Actually placing the restock order for Ticket 101

Missing capability: there is no MCP tool to create a vendor purchase order or shipment record of any kind — the schema itself has no purchase-order table, and the shop's rules explicitly forbid contacting vendors.

Proposed tool: create_vendor_order(vendor_id, sku, size, qty, reason), writing to a new, explicitly-scoped order-tracking table (not reusing invoices, since an order isn't yet a bill). It should require the same approval-gated pattern as payments — a create_order_approval_request / human-approved / single-use flow — since committing the shop to a purchase is a real financial exposure, just like a payment.

Agent: Extend Inventory's role rather than invent a new agent — Inventory already owns vendor-lead-time reasoning; it just needs a gated write capability it doesn't have today.

Safeguards: the same unpaid-invoice gate already enforced for shipment eligibility, plus a human approval step before any order is placed, and a hard requirement that the tool never actually contacts the vendor electronically — it should only create an internal record a human then acts on outside the system, at least until the shop is comfortable with real vendor API integration.

B. Actually getting Yale AI Club's answer on Ticket 103

Missing capability: no customer-communication channel exists at all — not even a one-way draft-and-log tool, let alone two-way correspondence.

Proposed tool: draft_customer_response(ticket_id, message) that only ever writes a draft to a reviewable queue (never sends anything), plus, if two-way communication is ever wanted, a strictly human-gated send_customer_message tool that requires the same kind of explicit, out-of-band human confirmation as a payment — and should probably never be callable by an agent directly at all, only by a human clicking "send" on a drafted message, mirroring exactly how payment approval works today.

Agent: Customer Service is the natural owner, but it needs this capability added — right now it can only reason in free text, with nothing to actually hand the result to a human in a structured, actionable way.

Safeguards: privacy matters here in a way it doesn't for the internal financial tools — any customer-communication tool needs to avoid leaking other customers' data from the tickets table, and a sent-message log should be append-only, the same pattern already used for the audit trail.

C. A multi-day cash-flow forecast across several pending obligations

Scenario: With $160 left after Problem 9's two payments, the next legitimate question is whether the shop can cover several upcoming obligations (another rent cycle, a restock, a new invoice) without going negative — a question none of the current tools answer, since they only report a point-in-time balance.

Missing capability: no tool projects cash forward against known future obligations; get_cash_balance only returns today's number.

Proposed tool: forecast_cash(horizon_days), reading leases.next_due, any open invoices, and recent payment cadence, returning a day-by-day projected balance — read-only, no new financial risk, but genuinely new analytical surface.

Agent: This fits Accounting's existing role; it doesn't need a new agent, just a new read-only tool.

Safeguards: none beyond the read-only guarantee itself — but the output should be clearly labeled as a projection, not a commitment, so a human doesn't mistake a forecast for a guarantee the way they might mistake Accounting's prose recommendation for an executed action.

6. Synthesis

The biggest thing this experiment taught me is that a multi-agent system's value doesn't show up evenly across tickets. On Ticket 102, five specialized agents bought me nothing a single agent with the same tools couldn't have done in one turn — the problem was just too simple to need division of labor. On Ticket 103, the multi-agent structure produced something a single agent genuinely might not have: Accounting voluntarily declining to make a pricing call outside its stated role and handing it back. That's the real argument for separation of responsibilities — not efficiency, but the fact that a narrowly-scoped agent is less likely to overreach than a broadly-scoped one, even though narrow scoping also costs you redundant tool calls (Ticket 103's double inventory check is the receipt for that cost).

The harder lesson is about authority versus capability. Every agent in this system is technically capable of reasoning its way to "we should pay this" — Accounting did exactly that on both financial tickets. What actually prevented an unauthorized payment wasn't the agents' good judgment; it was that record_approved_payment structurally cannot succeed without a real, human-created approval_id that no agent has a tool to generate for itself. That distinction — capability versus authority — is the single thing I'd insist a real business verify before trusting a system like this with real money: not "does the AI reason correctly," but "is there a hard boundary, external to the agent, that it cannot talk its way around."

Second, the missing customer-communication and vendor-ordering capabilities aren't just gaps, they're the reason two of three tickets couldn't close — a real deployment needs at least the draft-and-review version of both, even if sending stays human-gated indefinitely.
